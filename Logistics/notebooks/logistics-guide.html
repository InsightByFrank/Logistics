Foundations

Understanding the Three Datasets

Three separate CSV exports, each telling a different part of the same supply chain story — with no shared ID connecting them directly.

customer.csv (750 rows)

One row per customer order — acquisition cost, order value, payment timing, satisfaction, and support activity. In this export, every customer has placed exactly one order, so customer grain and order grain happen to coincide.

shipment.csv (728 rows)

One row per shipment — origin and destination city/country, product category, value, freight cost, customs clearance time, and delivery status.

logistics_performance.csv (100 rows)

One row per carrier, per region, per day — shipment volume, delay hours, fuel price, warehouse utilization, and damage claims. A short, dense operational snapshot (25 June days × 4 carrier-region pairs).

Three Files, No Shared Business Key — and That's Fine None of the three files share a common ID — no customer_id in shipment, no shipment_id in the logistics file. The only thing every file has in common is a date. That's still a legitimate model — it's called a fact constellation: multiple independent fact tables that share a conformed Calendar dimension but otherwise keep their own private dimensions. It's a looser structure than the earlier Finance guide's model (where two fact tables shared three conformed dimensions), and that's the correct call here — forcing a fake relationship between tables that don't actually share a business key would be worse than admitting they're genuinely separate.

Two Things Worth Knowing Before Modeling

  • The date ranges don't match. Customer order dates run from Jan 2024 all the way to Apr 2029 (six full years); shipment dates cover Jan 2024–Dec 2025 (two years); logistics data covers just 25 days in June 2024. The shared Calendar table needs to span the widest of the three — 2024 through 2029 — even though any one fact table only populates part of it.
  • Days-to-payment is remarkably consistent — averaging almost exactly 40 days with very little variation (standard deviation under half a day). That's a strong signal this dataset simulates a fixed Net-40 payment term rather than organic, messy real-world payment timing. Worth knowing so the "Avg Days to Payment" KPI is read correctly — small movements away from 40 are the signal, not the noise.

Troubleshooting

Data Quality Case Study — The Singapore Column Shift

A fully diagnosable, fully fixable bug hiding in 24 rows of shipment.csv — and the exact method used to find it.

The Symptom

Three things looked wrong in shipment.csv, and at first glance they looked like three separate problems: delivery_status was blank on 24 rows; customs_clearance_time_days held the text "On-Time" or "Delayed" instead of a number on those same rows; and D_Country held a large number (like 65000) instead of a country name, also on those same rows.

The Diagnosis

Checking whether the three problems shared the exact same 24 row numbers confirmed they weren't three bugs — they were one bug, with a cascading effect:

null delivery_status rows:        24
non-numeric customs_clearance rows: 24
numeric D_Country rows:             24
→ all three are the exact same 24 rows

And every one of those 24 rows shares one more thing in common: the destination city is Singapore, every single time — not a random scattering of destinations, all of them. That's the root cause. Singapore is both a city and a country with the same name. Whatever process generated this file appears to have left D_Country blank specifically when the destination country name would have exactly duplicated the destination city name — and a blank cell, rather than being preserved as an empty value, caused every column after it to shift one position to the left to fill the gap:

ColumnShould HoldActually Holds (Shifted)
D_Country"Singapore"(blank — the missing value that caused the shift)
valuee.g. 65,000Correct — this one wasn't shifted
freight_coste.g. 3,250Holds what should be in value
customs_clearance_time_dayse.g. 2.2Holds what should be in freight_cost
delivery_status"On-Time"Holds what should be in customs_clearance_time_days, and "On-Time"/"Delayed" ends up one column early, in customs_clearance_time_days's slot — leaving delivery_status itself blank
A Second, Independent Way to Confirm It Across the clean rows, freight_cost sits at almost exactly 5% of value — reliably enough that it works as a sanity check on its own. Run that same ratio on the 24 suspect rows using their current (shifted) values and it comes out wildly wrong; run it using the reconstructed values and it lands right back at ~5%. Two independent checks agreeing is a strong confirmation that the diagnosis — and the fix — are correct, not just plausible.

The Fix

In Power Query, for the 24 affected rows only (filtered where delivery_status is null):

  1. Set D_Country to "Singapore" — recoverable with certainty, since the destination city is already known and Singapore's city and country names are identical.
  2. Shift every value one column to the right: value ← current D_Country (the numeric one); freight_cost ← current value; customs_clearance_time_days ← current freight_cost; delivery_status ← current customs_clearance_time_days (the text one).
  3. Verify the freight-to-value ratio on the corrected rows now sits near 5%, consistent with the rest of the file.

This is the same discipline as every prior data-quality catch in this series — LinkedIn/LinkedIn Ads, the Budget-vs-Actual fan-out, Contract_ID's broken key — the fix only works because the diagnosis was specific enough to state exactly which rows, exactly why, and exactly what the correct values should have been, rather than just "some rows look wrong."

A Small Dimension That's Worth Building Even With Only an ID

Dim_Supplier here holds nothing but seven codes — SUP-A through SUP-G — no name, no country, no other attribute to enrich it with. It's still worth building as its own table rather than leaving supplier_id flat in the fact table: with 750 orders spread across only 7 suppliers, the de-duplication payoff is real (roughly 107 rows collapsed into 1), and having a dedicated table now means if supplier names or regions get added to the source later, there's already a home for them. The value of a dimension table isn't only about how rich its attributes are — cardinality alone can justify it.

Two Role-Playing Dimensions, Two Different Reasons

This model has two separate instances of the same modeling pattern seen with Admission/Discharge dates in the Healthcare guide — a single dimension related to one fact table more than once, because that fact table has more than one column pointing at the same kind of thing.

Calendar, Related Twice on Fact_Orders

order_date (active relationship) and payment_date (inactive, activated via USERELATIONSHIP()) both point at Calendar[Date]. Exactly the Admission/Discharge pattern, just renamed for a sales context.

Dim_Location, Related Twice on Fact_Shipments

Every shipment has an origin city and a destination city — both are "a location," so both relate to the same Dim_Location table rather than needing two separate city tables. Origin stays active; Destination stays inactive, activated with USERELATIONSHIP() whenever a measure needs to analyze by destination instead of origin.

The Pattern Generalizes Beyond Dates Role-playing dimensions get introduced through dates because that's the most common case — but the actual rule is broader: any time one fact table has two or more columns that all conceptually point at "the same kind of thing," that thing gets one dimension table, related more than once, rather than two duplicate tables. Origin and Destination are both cities; Order Date and Payment Date are both dates. Recognizing "these two columns are really the same category of information" is the skill — the specific category doesn't have to be a calendar.

DAX — Calendar (shared across all three fact tables) 
Calendar =
ADDCOLUMNS (
    CALENDAR ( DATE ( 2024, 1, 1 ), DATE ( 2029, 12, 31 ) ),
    "Year", YEAR ( [Date] ),
    "Quarter", "Q" & QUARTER ( [Date] ),
    "MonthNumber", MONTH ( [Date] ),
    "MonthName", FORMAT ( [Date], "MMMM" ),
    "MonthShort", FORMAT ( [Date], "MMM" ),
    "YearMonth", FORMAT ( [Date], "YYYY-MM" )
)

Notice the range: 2024 through 2029, sized to the widest of the three fact tables' date spans (Customer orders), even though Shipments only needs through 2025 and Logistics only needs 25 days in June 2024. A shared Calendar has to be sized for whichever fact table needs the most room — not the one you happened to model first.

An Observation Worth a Judgment Call: Dim_Carrier and Dim_Region

Checking the data shows every carrier operates in exactly one region — FastShip and ReliableLogistics are always North America, GlobalConnect is always Europe, OceanExpress is always Asia-Pacific. That's a clean functional dependency, which technically means Region could just become an attribute inside Dim_Carrier instead of its own table. The choice made here is to keep them separate anyway: Region is a broader geographic concept that a real business would likely want to filter and report on independently of carrier (and a carrier expanding into a second region next quarter would break a collapsed table's assumption immediately). Not every clean functional dependency has to be collapsed — sometimes the more durable model keeps two dimensions apart even when today's data would technically allow merging them.

Customer & Order Performance

How well are we acquiring, satisfying, and getting paid by customers?

DAX — Core & Sub-KPI Measures 
Total Order Value = SUM ( Fact_Orders[Order Value USD] )
Avg Order Value = AVERAGE ( Fact_Orders[Order Value USD] )

Total Acquisition Cost = SUM ( Fact_Orders[Acquisition Cost USD] )
Order Value per Acquisition $ = DIVIDE ( [Total Order Value], [Total Acquisition Cost] )

Avg Satisfaction Score = AVERAGE ( Fact_Orders[Satisfaction Score] )
% Top Satisfaction (Score = 5) =
DIVIDE (
    CALCULATE ( COUNTROWS ( Fact_Orders ), Fact_Orders[Satisfaction Score] = 5 ),
    COUNTROWS ( Fact_Orders )
)

Avg Days to Payment = AVERAGE ( Fact_Orders[Days To Payment] )
Days to Payment vs 40-Day Term = [Avg Days to Payment] - 40

Total Support Tickets = SUM ( Fact_Orders[Support Tickets] )
% Orders With a Support Ticket =
DIVIDE (
    CALCULATE ( COUNTROWS ( Fact_Orders ), Fact_Orders[Support Tickets] > 0 ),
    COUNTROWS ( Fact_Orders )
)
Why "Order Value per Acquisition $" Is the Interesting Sub-KPI Here Total Acquisition Cost alone just says how much was spent finding customers — it doesn't say whether that spend is working. Dividing order value by acquisition cost turns two disconnected totals into a single efficiency ratio: "$18.60 of order value for every $1 spent acquiring the customer." That's the number a growth or marketing lead actually wants next to the raw spend figure — the same instinct behind every "context line" in this series, applied to a genuinely different business question than procurement's budget variance or finance's margin.

Shipment & Trade Flow

Where is freight moving, and is it arriving on time and on budget?

DAX — Core & Sub-KPI Measures 
Total Shipment Value = SUM ( Fact_Shipments[Value] )
Avg Shipment Value = AVERAGE ( Fact_Shipments[Value] )

Total Freight Cost = SUM ( Fact_Shipments[Freight Cost] )
Freight Cost % of Value = DIVIDE ( [Total Freight Cost], [Total Shipment Value] )

Total Shipments = COUNTROWS ( Fact_Shipments )
On-Time Shipments =
CALCULATE ( COUNTROWS ( Fact_Shipments ), Fact_Shipments[Delivery Status] = "On-Time" )
% On-Time Delivery = DIVIDE ( [On-Time Shipments], [Total Shipments] )
Delayed Shipments =
CALCULATE ( COUNTROWS ( Fact_Shipments ), Fact_Shipments[Delivery Status] = "Delayed" )

Avg Customs Clearance (Days) = AVERAGE ( Fact_Shipments[Customs Clearance Time Days] )
Max Customs Clearance (Days) = MAX ( Fact_Shipments[Customs Clearance Time Days] )

Export Value = CALCULATE ( [Total Shipment Value], Fact_Shipments[Type] = "Export" )
Export Share of Value % = DIVIDE ( [Export Value], [Total Shipment Value] )
Why "Freight Cost % of Value" Beats the Raw Freight Total Total Freight Cost by itself rises and falls with shipment volume — more shipments simply means more freight spend, which tells you nothing about efficiency. Expressing it as a percentage of shipment value turns it into a number that should stay roughly flat (around 5%, based on this data) regardless of volume — so if it ever drifts upward, that's a real signal about freight rates or routing, not just "we shipped more stuff this month."

A Different Map Technique Than the Procurement Guide The Procurement dashboard's map used explicit latitude/longitude for pin-drop precision. This dataset only provides country names, not coordinates — which calls for a Filled Map (choropleth) instead: drag Dim_Location[Country] (filtered to the Destination role via USERELATIONSHIP() in the measure, or by building the visual against a Destination-specific view) onto the Location field, and Total Shipment Value onto Color saturation — Power BI geocodes the country names automatically and shades each country by value. Two different map types exist in Power BI for two different kinds of location data: precise coordinates call for a point/bubble map; region or country names call for a filled map instead.

Advanced Layout & Chart Techniques, Round Two

Four specific corrections walked through in full — sidebar navigation, per-chart info/back icons, a KPI trend line that marks its own high and low, and shading a line chart only where it beats average.

1. A Rounded Sidebar With Icon-Only Page Navigation

This replaces a horizontal tab strip with a slim, floating vertical rail — common in modern SaaS dashboards, and a genuinely different navigation feel from a Page Navigator visual.

  1. Draw the sidebar shape first. Insert → Shapes → Rounded Rectangle, tall and narrow, large corner radius (drag the yellow corner handle most of the way in so it reads as a pill, not a rectangle with slightly soft corners). Fill it with the report's primary color, no border, and send it behind everything else on the page via the Selection pane.
  2. Add one icon button per report page. Insert → Icons (Power BI's own icon library, or any consistent icon set) — one small rounded square per page, stacked vertically inside the sidebar with even spacing. Recolor each icon to a muted tone against the sidebar so only the active page's icon stands out.
  3. Wire each icon to a page. Select an icon → Format pane → Action → turn on → Type: Page navigation → choose the destination page. Now clicking the icon behaves exactly like a page navigator tab, just styled as a single-purpose icon instead of a text pill.
  4. Highlight the active page automatically. Give each page's icon a background shape colored in the accent color, then hide/show that highlight shape per page using the Selection pane (each page only shows its own highlight) — or duplicate the sidebar once per page with the correct icon pre-highlighted, which is simpler to maintain even though it means one sidebar object per page instead of a single shared one.
Why Icon-Only, No Text Labels A sidebar this narrow doesn't have room for text labels without wrapping awkwardly — icons carry the meaning instead, at the cost of relying on the icon shape being intuitive (a person for Customers, a truck for Shipments). If an icon's meaning isn't obvious at a glance, a tooltip (Format pane → General → Tooltip → Type: Text, or a Report Page Tooltip for something richer) on the icon is worth adding so first-time users aren't guessing.

2. Info + Back Icons on Every Chart

Two small icons, consistently placed top-right of every chart card:

  • The info icon is the Report Page Tooltip technique already covered in the Procurement guide — a small "i" icon with its Format pane Tooltip set to a dedicated tooltip page explaining that specific chart. Repeat it on every chart card, each pointing to its own tooltip page (or a shared one, if the explanation is generic).
  • The back icon only needs to do something on charts that have drill-through or drill-down enabled. For a chart with a drill-through target page, Power BI already auto-inserts a Back button on the destination page — the icon on the source chart itself is more of a visual promise ("this chart goes somewhere else") than a functioning control on that card specifically. For a chart with drill-down (Category → Sub-Category, for instance), the small circular arrows that appear in a chart's own header when you drill down are the real back control — Format pane → Visual header → make sure "Drill up" stays visible, and it renders in the chart's own corner automatically once you've drilled into a level.
Match the Icon to What the Chart Actually Does Don't place a back icon on a chart that has no drill-through and no drill-down — it will look clickable and do nothing, which is worse than not having the icon at all. Add it only where a real navigation action exists behind it, and let its presence signal that action truthfully.

3. A KPI Trend Line That Marks Its Own High and Low

The sparkline underneath each KPI number carries three things: the trend line itself, a thin dashed green line at the period's highest value, and a thin dashed red line at its lowest — with a small colored dot marking exactly where each occurs.

DAX 
-- Example shown for Total Order Value; the same three-measure
-- pattern applies to any KPI's mini trend chart.

Order Value by Month = 
CALCULATE ( [Total Order Value], ALLSELECTED ( Calendar[YearMonth] ) )

Order Value Period High =
CALCULATE ( MAXX ( ALLSELECTED ( Calendar[YearMonth] ), [Total Order Value] ) )

Order Value Period Low =
CALCULATE ( MINX ( ALLSELECTED ( Calendar[YearMonth] ), [Total Order Value] ) )
  1. Build a small Line Chart visual (not the table-embedded native sparkline, which doesn't support this level of custom coloring) sized down to sit directly under the KPI card — X axis: Calendar[YearMonth], Y axis: Order Value by Month.
  2. Add two constant lines via the Analytics pane: one bound to Order Value Period High (color it green), one bound to Order Value Period Low (color it red). Both render as thin dashed horizontal lines spanning the chart.
  3. Strip every native chart element — axes, gridlines, title, legend — off in the Format pane, so only the three lines remain, then resize the visual down to sparkline scale and position it directly beneath the KPI number.

Because the high/low measures use ALLSELECTED() rather than a hardcoded date range, both reference lines automatically move with whatever period the page's slicers currently show — the "high" and "low" always describe the period actually in view, not a fixed historical high that stops being meaningful once the data moves past it.

4. Shading Only the Above-Average Portion of a Line Chart

Covered in full in the Procurement guide's Signature Techniques section — the same technique applies here unchanged, and is worth restating for a guide meant to stand on its own:

DAX 
Category Avg Line =
AVERAGEX ( ALLSELECTED ( Calendar[YearMonth] ), [Total Order Value] )

Above Average Value =
IF ( [Total Order Value] > [Category Avg Line], [Total Order Value], BLANK () )

Below Average Value =
IF ( [Total Order Value] <= [Category Avg Line], [Total Order Value], BLANK () )

Plot all three — the raw value (thin line, always drawn, no fill, for visual continuity), Above Average Value, and Below Average Value — on the same Line and Area chart. Turn Area shading on for Above Average Value only, colored in the secondary or accent color; turn it off for Below Average Value. BLANK() creates a gap rather than a zero, so the fill only appears exactly where the line genuinely sits above its own average — which is what the "with the above-average stretch shaded" note on every hero chart in the wireframes above is describing.

5. Bar/Column Charts With a Target Marker (Bullet Chart)

The reference image shows each bar paired with a vertical tick mark (the planned/target value) and a percentage variance label at the end — a distinct chart type, not a formatting option on a standard bar chart.

  1. Get the Bullet Chart visual. Insert → Get more visuals → search "Bullet Chart" (the free version published by Data Viz / OKViz is the common choice) → Add.
  2. Fill the three core fields: Category (e.g. Dim_MarketSegment[Market Segment]), Value (the actual measure — e.g. Total Orders), and Target Value (the planned/target measure — e.g. Target Orders, a measure you define however "target" is calculated for your data, whether a fixed budget figure or a prior-period comparison). The visual renders the bar and the tick mark from these three fields alone.
  3. Add the % variance label. The Bullet Chart visual's Format pane includes a "Labels" section that can display the value, but for a custom "+7.8%" style label matching the reference exactly, build it as its own measure and place it via the visual's "Comparison label" or "Extra Data Labels" option (naming varies by visual version) — or, if the visual's built-in options don't offer enough control, overlay a small separate Table/Card visual showing just that percentage, positioned at the end of each bar:
DAX 
Target Orders = [some fixed or prior-period benchmark measure]

Orders vs Target % =
DIVIDE ( [Total Orders] - [Target Orders], [Target Orders] )

Conditional formatting on that label measure (positive → green text, negative → the report's muted "unfavorable" tone rather than a harsh red, matching the reference's soft beige for negative values rather than alarm-red) finishes the look.

Why a Custom Visual Instead of a Native Bar Chart Power BI's native bar and column charts have no field well for a second "target" value that renders as a marker rather than a second bar — Reference Lines in the Analytics pane draw one line across the whole chart, not a different target value per category. When the requirement is genuinely "each bar needs its own individual target marker," that's the signal to reach for a purpose-built visual rather than trying to force the native one into a shape it wasn't designed for — the same judgment call as the Icon Map visual in the Procurement guide.

Wireframing the Three Pages

Canvas 1920 × 1080, built in PowerPoint — a magazine-style layout this time, with empty chart containers standing in for the real visuals.

A Different Layout on Purpose — Now With a Sidebar

This is the corrected version of the wireframe, built after the first pass: a floating, rounded icon-only sidebar now handles page navigation (Technique 1 above) rather than a top tab strip — one small icon per dashboard page, the active one highlighted in the accent color. Each page still follows a hero + sidebar-column + wide footer chart structure, but the sidebar column now holds three stacked charts instead of two, bringing every page up to five charts total. Every chart card carries its own info and back icon pair (Technique 2), and every KPI card now shows its own mini trend line with green/red high-low markers (Technique 3) instead of a plain number.

Empty Containers, Not Chart Mockups

Each of the five main chart cards still shows only its pill tag, title, the business question it answers, and a dashed placeholder box labeled with the chart type — no drawn bars, lines, or donuts. The KPI mini trend charts are the one exception, since the high/low-marker technique is itself part of what's being planned here, not a stand-in for a visual that doesn't exist yet.
